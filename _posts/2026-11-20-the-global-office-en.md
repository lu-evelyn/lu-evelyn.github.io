Richard Baldwin (IMD, CEPR, and NBER), “The Global Office: How Globalisation’s Frontier is Shifting from Factories to Offices,” [NBER Working Paper 35975](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35975), October 2026. This is a working paper and has not been peer reviewed. Its main sources are two public OECD databases. Trade in Value Added (TiVA) covers 80 economies from 1995 to 2024, with OECD projections for 2023 and 2024. Trade in Employment (TiM) covers 68 economies from 1995 to 2022. Sébastien Miroudot of the OECD supplied the full sets of tables in current and previous-year prices. These notes follow the official NBER PDF, which has 35 pages of main text plus Appendices A–C. Page references accompany the figures and tables below.

Baldwin developed the framework that divides globalization into three unbundlings. The first moved goods across borders. Cheaper shipping separated production from consumption. The second moved parts of factories across borders. Communications technology made it possible to coordinate at a distance, so firms could break a production line into stages and move some of them to lower-wage countries. The result was the familiar global factory. The third moves office work across borders. People doing accounting, software, legal drafting, or customer support stay where they are and send their work over the internet. Baldwin calls this international division of labor in services the global office.

## What crosses borders changed after 2008

Globalization brings to mind shipping containers, ports, and assembly lines. Baldwin opens by saying that picture is out of date. Before 2008, both manufactured goods and services exports grew rapidly. Manufacturing slowed after 2008, while services kept growing. From 2008 to 2024, global manufactured export volumes rose 53%, services exports rose 93%, and exports of digitally deliverable services rose 137%.

“Digitally deliverable services” is a standard statistical category used by international organizations. It covers information and communications, finance and insurance, professional and technical services, and administrative and support services. All can deliver their output over a network. The official category also includes real estate, which is large in GDP, mostly due to imputed housing rents, but barely shows up in trade. The paper removes it from the measure used in the rest of the analysis and calls the result DDSx. “Office” below refers to that measure.

Intermediate inputs are the clue to what has changed. An export used by a foreign firm as an input is supply-chain trade, a sign of a global value chain. Between 2008 and 2024, intermediate exports of digitally deliverable services rose 113%, while manufactured intermediates rose 46%. Business-to-business service supply chains are growing rapidly alongside services sold directly to consumers. This leads to Baldwin's central claim that globalization's frontier is shifting from factories to offices.

General Electric illustrates how office work crosses borders. In 1997, GE set up Gecis in Gurgaon, India, to handle paperwork for the parent company. This was a global capability center, an overseas unit that performed office work for its own multinational. Gecis later spun off as Genpact and began selling business process outsourcing to outside clients. Cross-border office work now ranges from back-office data processing to software development, cybersecurity, equity research, and legal drafting. It is done by multinational subsidiaries, outsourcing firms, professional networks, and individual freelancers. They share one feature. The work crosses borders while the people stay put.

The paper offers an informal model of why services differ from manufacturing, five comparisons using a common data framework, and implications for development and the future of trade. The comparisons cover size and growth, jobs and pay, the shape of trade networks, distance and time zones, and the contribution to growth in developing economies. Baldwin calls the informal account a “vibe model,” meant to capture the pattern without a formal derivation.

## Why offices need a different model

Classic offshoring theory was designed around factories. In Grossman and Rossi-Hansberg's model, production is a sequence of tasks, each with its own fixed cost of moving it abroad. Firms rank tasks by how hard they are to move and cut the list in two. Easy-to-move tasks go to lower-wage countries, while the others stay home. Behind that logic are standardized products, sequential processes, and machinery at the center of production with interchangeable workers. Moving a factory means moving the machines, the layout of the production line, and the quality-control procedures, then staffing it with workers in another country.

Baldwin argues that offices work differently on all three counts. The value of a marketing report lies in what makes it particular. Its inputs, including data, examples, interviews, and the regulatory context, vary as the work progresses. Diagnosing the problem, developing a response, revising it, and talking through it with others recur throughout the process. People are at the center. In the office, machines help people do their work. In the factory, people help machines do theirs.

The main change in the vibe model is that the cost of moving an office task depends on where related tasks are done. Baldwin gives the example of an analyst who relies on both prepared data and a senior colleague's judgment. Splitting tightly linked tasks across two countries makes them costlier to coordinate. The decision then concerns a bundle of tasks, with the firm weighing which tasks to move together. The office example below builds on the analyst in the paper.

```text
Factory tasks form a sequence, with a cut based on offshoring costs.
  [Stamping] → [Welding] → [Painting] → [Assembly] | [R&D] [Sales]
  <---------- Move to a lower-wage country -------->   <--- Stay --->

Office tasks are linked and can move in bundles.
       [Data preparation] ─── [Modeling]
             |       \            |
             |        \           |
       [Senior judgment] ───── [Client communication]
                                (stays in the client's country)

Move data preparation alone, and each of its links crosses a border.
Move data preparation and modeling together, and their link stays local.
```

Suppose the wage gap is large for data preparation and small for modeling. Moving data preparation by itself could cost as much in new cross-border coordination with modeling, senior colleagues, and clients as it saves in wages. Moving modeling too keeps the most frequent interaction in one place. Only the links to senior colleagues and clients still cross borders. Even if moving modeling saves very little on its own, the bundle can pay off. In the paper, a high-skilled task with a small or even negative wage gap may move because doing so makes it worthwhile to move many related lower-skilled tasks. Once some tasks move, the firm must recalculate the cost of each bundle as new cross-border links take shape.

The model makes two other changes that matter for the data. Office processes are irregular, so coordination costs are hard to know in advance, and reorganizing entails sunk costs. Firms tend to wait. More uncertainty widens the range over which they sit tight, making existing arrangements more durable. Then there is distance. Sending bytes a kilometer costs nearly the same as sending them ten thousand kilometers. Time zones matter more when teams need to be online together to meet and talk. They can also help when teams can work in shifts. A Geneva team can send a first draft of a slide deck to Bangkok at the end of its day and get the revised deck back before work begins the next morning. Time differences make synchronous work harder and can speed up work that can be handed off.

Baldwin presents the model as a way to organize the argument. It has no formal solution or direct empirical test in the paper. The data below are examined for patterns consistent with it.

## Offices export a smaller share of output, with faster growth and more net jobs

The two employment measures assign jobs differently. Export-linked employment counts a job against the industry that exports the final product. Trade-linked employment asks which industry's workers meet foreign final demand. An exported car includes design, software, and financial services. The first measure counts those jobs in manufacturing and the second counts them in services. Both measures point to offices as the source of the net increase in trade-related jobs from 2008 to 2022. On the export-linked measure, digitally deliverable services added around 17 million jobs, against a global net gain of around 15 million. Manufacturing lost around 10 million.

On pay, the global averages are about $32,000 per trade-linked office job and $19,000 per factory job. In advanced economies, the two are close, at around $59,000 each. The global gap largely reflects where the jobs are. In advanced economies there are 43% of all trade-linked office jobs and only 19% of the corresponding factory jobs. China adds another illustration. Office jobs pay 11% more overall, almost entirely because of finance. Professional and business services, the largest employer within the office category, pays around $11,000 per worker, below about $14,000 in manufacturing. Pay varies much more across specific industries than between the two broad categories.

Could higher prices explain the faster growth in services exports? Using the new previous-year price data, Baldwin finds that office export prices rose about 23% relative to manufactured goods from 1995 to 2024. Most of that happened before 2003, with little trend since. Manufacturing price indexes make more adjustments for quality, especially for electronics, which could explain some of the measured gap. Crucially, office exports still grew faster in real volumes after adjusting for prices.

The difference is sharper over time. From 1995 to 2022, China's share of global manufacturing intermediate exports rose from 2% to 18%, and the share of flows with at least one end in Asia rose from 36% to 55%. Manufacturing's center of gravity moved east. Trade in intermediate services grew roughly sevenfold over the same period, while the hub-and-spoke network held its shape. The U.S. share fell from about 20% to 17%, still ranking first. The share of flows with at least one end in Asia barely moved, from 33% to 34%.

Ireland presents a complication. Its share of intermediate services exports rose from less than 1% to 8.5%, largely because of multinationals' intellectual property and tax arrangements. Exports can rise on paper even if the office work stays elsewhere. Baldwin keeps Ireland, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg in the figures and also runs the numbers without them. With the three removed, the U.S. share of intermediate services exports rises from 17.0% to 18.4%, its import share rises from 11.0% to 13.8%, and it remains first on both measures (Table B.8, p. 57).

Baldwin offers three explanations for the different maps, all consistent with the data and individually untested in the paper. First, the cost of moving containers favors regional manufacturing clusters, while moving bytes is cheap across regions. Second, the United States has stayed at the center. An unpublished model by Kleinman and Yerushalmi suggests that digital capital, such as software, data, and intellectual property, can accumulate and be used repeatedly, so an early lead reinforces itself. The sunk costs discussed in the vibe model may also keep the existing network in place. Third, manufacturing moved east while office work did not. In economic geography, industries with strong scale economies can suddenly concentrate in one place once transport costs cross a threshold. With strong scale economies, subsidies, and asymmetric market protection, a previously smaller country can become the manufacturing center. Baldwin thinks China's rise fits that account. Services tend to have weaker scale economies and are less prone to that sort of shift. In 2024, China produced 34% of global manufacturing output and the United States produced 36% of global office-services output. Their positions are comparable in size, reached through different paths.

## Kilometers matter less than time zones

The fourth finding comes from a regression. Economists often use a gravity equation to describe trade between countries. Larger economies tend to trade more with each other, and countries farther apart tend to trade less. Baldwin uses the same data source and the same 80 economies to estimate exports separately for manufacturing and office services. The sample covers 176,960 country-pair and year combinations from 1995 to 2022. The regressions control for characteristics of each exporter and importer by year and for shared borders, languages, and colonial ties. The question is how much trade varies with distance and with time differences when the other conditions are held constant.

| Specification (Table C1, p. 60) | Total manufacturing exports | Total office-services exports |
|---|---|---|
| Distance only, coefficient on log distance | −0.767 | −0.567 |
| With time zones, coefficient on log distance | −0.830 | −0.435 |
| With time zones, coefficient per 1-hour increase in time difference | 0.019 (not statistically significant) | −0.043 |

The numbers shown are point estimates in log units, with standard errors in the original table. On the first specification, doubling distance is associated with about 41% fewer manufactured exports and 33% fewer office-services exports. The difference is statistically significant. Adding time zones lowers the estimated sensitivity of office services to distance. Each additional hour of time difference is associated with around 4% fewer office-services exports. Eight hours implies about 29% less. The time-zone coefficient for total manufactured exports is statistically insignificant.

That fits the vibe model. Office teams benefit from overlapping work hours, while containers move regardless of the time difference. Splitting the estimates into time-zone bands, Baldwin finds the largest negative association for office services at seven to nine hours. An eight-hour gap is exactly where two nine-to-five workdays stop overlapping. Beyond nine hours, the estimate is less negative, possibly because more of those transactions involve work that can be handed off. The rebound falls short of significance at the 5% level (p = 0.063), and Baldwin treats it as suggestive.

Baldwin sets out three important limitations. First, time difference and distance are highly correlated (0.80). Their effects can be separated only through differences across country pairs, regardless of functional form. Second, regional comparative advantage may be correlated with time zones. Production can be concentrated to the east and consumption to the west in ways the fixed effects do not fully absorb. Third, the regressions omit regional trade agreements. The estimates are conditional associations, and interpreting them as causal coordination costs would go beyond the design.

## Export-led growth is shifting toward services, with modest contributions so far

The fifth finding concerns developing countries. Manufacturing exports helped East Asia catch up in recent decades. Is that route still open? Baldwin defines an industry as export-led when the real domestic value added embodied in its exports grows faster than the economy as a whole. He labels the services case SLED and the manufacturing case MLED.

Among 43 emerging economies, there is a clear before-and-after pattern around 2008. The number in which manufacturing export value added grew faster than the overall economy fell from 36 to 17. For office services it rose from 31 to 37. The number led by manufacturing alone fell from 11 to just one, Vietnam. On this test, services-led export growth has become the norm.

Baldwin offers two qualifications. The shift is relative. The median growth rate of office-services export value added fell from 8.6% a year to 6.3%, while manufacturing dropped from 7.4% to 3.0%. Manufacturing slowed more. A sector can also grow quickly from a very small base. In Nigeria after 2008, office-services export value added grew 19 percentage points faster per year than the overall economy, but in 2008 it was just 0.05% of national value added.

He then measures the economic weight of exports in percentage points of annual growth. The median contribution of office services was 0.12 percentage points a year before 2008 and 0.09 afterward, when median growth across these economies was 4.7% and 3.6%, respectively. The median contribution of manufacturing fell from 0.70 to 0.37 percentage points. In only four economies was the services contribution sizable and greater than manufacturing's. Those were the United Arab Emirates (0.64), the Philippines (0.60), Costa Rica (0.54), and São Tomé and Príncipe (0.45). India is especially interesting. Office services contributed 0.90 percentage points a year before 2008, above manufacturing's 0.62 and about one-eighth of India's 7.2% annual growth. After 2008 the figure fell to 0.43, below manufacturing's 0.64, and office-services export value added leveled off at around 6% of the economy. On the contribution measure, India's growth was office-led before 2008 and ceased being so afterward.

## Baldwin's conjectures and how far the paper goes

Baldwin closes with an explicit conjecture that the future of trade is in services. He gives three reasons. First, the cost of coordinating office work across borders continues to fall. Large language models and simultaneous translation could make it easier to split up work that previously had to happen in the same room. Offices export 13% of their output, compared with 22% for factories, leaving plenty of room for growth. Second, geopolitics is slowing factory globalization. Baldwin expects less effect on office work because the winners from services outsourcing (service firms) are better organized than the losers (office workers in advanced economies). Third, the digital capital supporting the U.S. hub can be used repeatedly. New exporters can draw on it and join the network as new spokes. Baldwin expects more of this development at the city level, as with Bengaluru. He names Bogotá, Nairobi, Cape Town, and Jakarta as candidates.

The five empirical patterns above come from reproducible OECD data and the author's calculations and hold up when he excludes the three tax hubs, changes distance and time-zone measures, and divides the sample by period. The predictions about language models, geopolitics, and city-level services booms remain untested. The paper is descriptive. Baldwin repeatedly says the growth contributions are a statistical decomposition. The data on services trade are weaker than the data on goods, and 2023 and 2024 are projections. Employment and pay end in 2022, and the compensation measure counts the self-employed in headcount without including their earnings. One further possibility is ours. Large language models may substitute for offshore office workers directly as well as reduce coordination costs. That possibility matters for whether lower coordination costs ultimately favor offshoring or automation at home.

Return to the opening question. Globalization has kept moving, and the source of growth has changed. After 2008, the expansion of cross-border supply chains and the net gains in trade jobs came from office services. The global office has a different structure from the global factory. It has stayed centered on the United States for decades. Time zones matter more, kilometers less. It offers developing countries another route, though so far only a few have seen a sizable contribution to growth. The next time you hear about deglobalization, ask which flows are slowing. Are we talking about boxes or bytes?
