Asif A. Ghazanfar wrote “How to Turn a Philanderer Into a Father” for issue 16 of *Asterisk Magazine*, dated November 3, 2026. He studies primate and human behavior and its neural basis, and says he has worked with fish, rats, lemurs, several kinds of monkeys, and humans. The essay draws on existing research in animal behavior, neuroendocrinology, and anthropology. These notes distinguish the studies he cites, his own interpretation, and where that evidence runs out.

## What is the essay asking?

Ghazanfar opens with a story about his own family. He suggested that his single younger brother get a dog. Walking a dog, he reasoned, signals to potential partners that you would make a responsible father. That leads to his question. In the animal kingdom, plenty of males fertilize eggs, but few stay to care for their offspring, especially among mammals. Why is paternal care so rare, and what makes it appear in a species or an individual?

His answer is that most male brains already have a circuit for paternal care. Pair bonding, hormones, scent, and the environment a male grows up in can switch it on. In humans, culture adds another layer.

## The first father was a fish

The most common reproductive strategy among fish involves external fertilization, after which the parents go their separate ways. Success comes from producing a great many eggs. The next most common pattern involves males guarding the eggs. If care includes any behavior that improves the offspring's chances of survival, Ghazanfar argues, male fish were likely the earliest caregivers.

The explanation comes down to cost. A male already defends a territory to attract females. Chasing predators away from eggs laid there takes little extra effort and still leaves him free to attract more females. Females are busy growing and producing more energetically costly eggs, with little reason to hold a territory. Ghazanfar describes an experiment with bluegill sunfish in which a male's decision to guard the eggs depended on how many there were. From the start, the payoff from providing care depended on the situation.

## Why did fathers become rarer on land?

Land animals evolved internal fertilization to keep eggs and sperm from drying out. Two paths then opened up. Some lay hard-shelled eggs. Mammals keep the embryo inside the mother and give birth to live young. Internal fertilization made it harder for a male to know which eggs he had fertilized. Gestation also opened up a long interval between mating and birth, during which the female could mate with other males.

Ghazanfar frames the male's choice in terms of time and energy. Leaving after mating opens up another mating opportunity. Staying means waiting for a birth when paternity may be uncertain. The figure given for paternal care among mammals is about 4–5%.

| | External fertilization in water | Internal fertilization and live birth on land |
|---|---|---|
| Can a male see fertilization? | Yes | No, so paternity is uncertain |
| Time from mating to birth | Very short | Long because of gestation |
| Cost of staying | Low, since he is already defending the territory | Lost opportunities to mate again |
| Common outcome | Male fish often guard eggs | Paternal care is about 4–5% among mammals |

The move onto land changed two parts of the decision to stay. Paternity became less certain, and waiting became more costly.

## Cohabitation, pair bonding, and the dormant MPOA circuit

The evidence at the center of the essay shifts the question toward what triggers paternal behavior.

Male house mice commonly kill pups, including their own. They cannot identify their own litter, and killing pups lets the female return to fertility sooner. When experimenters kept a male with a pregnant female after they mated, however, he cared for the pups when they were born about 18 days later. Longer cohabitation brought more care and less aggression. Ghazanfar reads this as the male's body using sustained contact to infer paternity in some way.

Some species arrive at a similar result through pair bonding. Mating is rewarding, spending time with that particular partner becomes rewarding too, and other potential mates become less appealing. Paternity becomes more certain. Two nearly identical-looking voles illustrate the difference. Prairie voles in harsh, resource-poor environments form pair bonds, and the males care for their offspring. Montane voles in better-resourced habitats leave after mating.

How widespread is the circuit for care, and how is it activated? Ghazanfar points to the medial preoptic area (MPOA), a small region of the hypothalamus. Lesions there sharply reduce maternal behavior in female rodents. Lesions in the same area also reduce paternal behavior in male California mice, a species in which fathers naturally provide care. Ghazanfar argues that evolution can readily retain similar circuitry in both sexes and activate it under different conditions.

The essay describes several cues, each backed by animal research.

- **Hormonal changes.** Pregnancy and delivery bring large hormonal shifts for mothers. In males, rises in estrogen or prolactin or a drop in testosterone may play a part. In male Mongolian gerbils and Campbell's dwarf hamsters, care is associated with a fall in testosterone after the pups are born.
- **Scents.** The vomeronasal organ in the nose detects pheromones in urine or amniotic fluid. Male California mice begin caring for pups after eating the placenta and maintain care by smelling their partner's urine. In one experiment, urine from a virgin, a nursing female, or a familiar female that the male had never mated with was less effective than his partner's.
- **Cues from the pups.** Their scents and calls can also play a role.
- **The placenta.** Male Campbell's dwarf hamsters even act as midwives, consuming amniotic fluid and placenta, both sources of hormones.

## How growing up alone or in a group changes behavior

The African striped mouse shows that the environment a male grows up in can move the switch in either direction.

Males may care for unrelated pups even without mating or forming a pair bond. After weaning, they may join an all-male bachelor group, stay where they were born, or live alone, depending on population density and resources. Males that grow up alone lick pups and keep them warm. Males from bachelor groups withhold care and often kill pups. Experiments reproduced these living conditions. Unmated, solitary males that cared for unfamiliar pups had substantially more neural activity in the MPOA than group-housed males that ignored or killed pups.

The mechanism Ghazanfar describes involves a gene called *Agouti*. It is expressed in the MPOA and produces a protein that dampens activity there. When researchers increased *Agouti* expression, males that had ignored pups began killing them, and males that had cared for pups began ignoring them. After a month of living alone, a group-housed male that previously withheld care became more willing to care for pups, and *Agouti* expression in the MPOA fell. The essay cites the 2026 *Nature* paper in a footnote.

```text
Population density and resources
  → Solitary life or a bachelor group
  → Agouti expression in the MPOA
  → MPOA activity (higher Agouti suppresses it)
  → Behavior from care to indifference to infanticide

A month alone → lower Agouti expression → more care
```

A male that joins a bachelor group in crowded conditions tends to have higher *Agouti* expression in the MPOA. Activity in the circuit is suppressed, and he ignores or attacks pups. Move him into solitary housing for a month, and expression falls, MPOA activity rises, and he starts caring for pups. The essay points to experiments for these steps. How social density actually alters *Agouti* expression remains an open question.

Ghazanfar's synthesis is that paternal behavior emerges from gene expression, neural circuitry, hormones, social conditions, and the environment at a particular time and place. Each contributes. In a footnote, he speculates that this flexibility may be common and go unseen because animals generally behave in typical ways in their usual environments. Biomedical studies also focus on very few species. The essay offers this as a hypothesis.

## Humans share the hormonal pattern and add culture

Ghazanfar begins with an anthropological example to show how much fatherhood can vary. Among the Aché of Paraguay, women and men can have multiple sexual partners. Men who had sex with a woman from the months before her pregnancy was recognized until the birth are understood to share paternity. They are distinguished as primary and secondary fathers. Children with both had higher survival rates. The fathers mainly provided protection and food, with little holding or playing. Ghazanfar notes that the arrangement produces more male investment precisely where biological paternity is least certain, challenging the familiar expectation that care rises with paternity certainty.

He then lays out hormonal evidence and the difficulty of establishing causation.

- Marmosets are cooperative breeders, as humans are. Marmoset fathers that carry their infants the most have the lowest testosterone.
- Human studies from the 1990s onward found lower testosterone in married men than in single men, higher levels after divorce, and lower levels in married fathers than in married men without children. Most of these were cross-sectional. Men with lower testosterone might simply have been more likely to marry and have children.
- A ten-year longitudinal study linked marriage to falling testosterone and divorce to rising levels.
- A 2011 longitudinal study followed hundreds of men in Cebu, Philippines. Single men with the highest initial testosterone were more likely to enter a relationship and become fathers. Testosterone fell after they partnered and had children, beyond what aging or stress could account for. Fathers of newborns experienced the largest declines, and fathers spending the most time on childcare had the lowest levels. Later research found that when time spent caring for children changed, testosterone shifted in the opposite direction.

| | Mating phase | Caregiving phase |
|---|---|---|
| Testosterone | High. Men with the highest levels while single were more likely to partner and become fathers. | Falls after partnering and fatherhood, with lower levels among men doing more childcare. |
| What it suggests | Supports mating effort | Supports caregiving |
| Evidence | Cebu longitudinal cohort in humans, plus studies in marmosets, gerbils, and other animals | Same evidence. Human brain-circuit evidence comes from fMRI, with limited resolution. |

The essay also sets out the limits of this pattern. Castrated rats stop killing pups but do not start caring for them. Several factors are at work beyond testosterone. The human MPOA is too small for existing MRI to reliably distinguish fathers from non-fathers in that particular region, so studies look at the broader hypothalamus and its connections. In fMRI studies, fathers and mothers use similar caregiving networks. Gay fathers, including fathers without a biological tie to the child, also activate the network. The more time they spend caring for a child on their own, the stronger the connections within it. Another study found that men with larger testes provided less care and showed weaker activation in a reward-related brain region while looking at pictures of their own children.

In humans, Ghazanfar says, more of the variation comes from culture and individual experience. Larger-brained primates can live in many kinds of social arrangements, which change how individuals find mates and in turn whether and how males provide care. He thinks variation within our species may exceed the variation across all other primate species. He returns to himself and his brother. They grew up with the same parents in the same Idaho town. One has been married for 25 years and has two daughters. The other is single and childless. Ghazanfar wonders whether caring for his one-year-old cousin outside Lahore one summer at the age of twelve changed how his circuitry developed during adolescence. He presents that as a personal guess.

## How far the evidence goes

The studies, Ghazanfar's interpretation, and his hypotheses carry different evidentiary weight.

- **Direct findings.** In several rodents, cohabitation, pair bonding, hormones, pheromones, and the environment they grow up in can change how males respond to pups. In striped mice, *Agouti* expression is associated with MPOA activity and caregiving, and changing expression experimentally changes behavior. In the Cebu longitudinal study, men's testosterone declined after they entered relationships and became fathers. Men who provided more care had lower levels.
- **Ghazanfar's synthesis.** Most males have a latent circuit for care that conditions can activate. Paternal behavior emerges from several interacting layers. MPOA lesions and cohabitation experiments with mice support the first idea, but the number of species tested is small. The second is an explanatory framework.
- **Hypotheses.** Behavioral flexibility may be common and hidden by ordinary conditions, and Ghazanfar's own path to fatherhood may have been shaped by caring for a baby in childhood. Both remain open questions.

The human evidence is largely correlational, and Ghazanfar acknowledges how difficult the causal relationships are to separate. The Aché example challenges the claim that care increases with paternity certainty. Whether the general pattern holds across more human societies remains unanswered.

## Returning to the opening question

Why do so few males provide paternal care, and what makes some start? In mammals, internal fertilization conceals paternity and pregnancy makes for a long wait. Males often gain little by staying, and the caregiving circuit usually remains dormant. Conditions that make paternity more certain or make staying more rewarding or natural can activate it. These include time together, pair bonding, hormonal changes, cues from partners and pups, and the social environment in which a male grows up. Human cultures add an extraordinary variety of arrangements. Ghazanfar's advice about getting a dog follows the same logic. Caring for a dog sends a signal to you and to other people that you are already taking care of someone. Whether that can actually change a person remains beyond the animal studies and human correlations discussed here.
