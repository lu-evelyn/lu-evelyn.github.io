## Why do some big government projects give rise to industries?

Governments periodically organize large R&D programs around a concrete problem, from a nuclear submarine to an air defense computer system or the Space Shuttle. These projects typically have a public mission. Some spawn an industry. Others achieve something remarkable and leave little commercial activity behind. Advocates of mission-oriented R&D in AI or energy often point to past successes. The question that gets less attention is what survives the project and what other companies can use.

Gross and Roche take up one of the most frequently cited cases, the U.S. radar program during World War II and the Radiation Laboratory at MIT, known as the Rad Lab. A little over thirty people attended its first meeting in November 1940. They knew almost nothing about how to turn the magnetron the British had brought, a device that produces high-power microwaves, into a radar system. By 1944 the lab had nearly 4,000 employees. By mid-1945 U.S. firms had produced more than $40 billion worth of radar equipment. The introduction says that nearly 200 U.S. companies were making radar systems or components by the early 1950s.

It is easy to tell this story as a direct line from wartime spending to a new industry. The paper turns that idea into something we can examine. Drawing on archives from the Rad Lab and its industrial partners, a directory of U.S. manufacturers, patent data, and engineering society directories, the authors follow three things into the postwar years and ask who used them. They trace knowledge written down, knowledge carried by people, and an industry structure divided between component suppliers and systems integrators. They propose coordinated R&D programs as a fourth organizational form alongside vertical integration within firms, industry consortia, and decentralized markets.

Written knowledge traveled widely. Knowledge embodied in people stayed closer to its source. Postwar specialization resembled the division of labor during the war. The evidence establishes that resemblance, while the question of direct inheritance remains open.

## Why a market alone struggled to assemble radar

A radar system transmits microwaves, receives their reflections, processes the signal, and presents it to the operator. All those steps have to be timed together. At the beginning of the war, almost none of the pieces existed. A radar set in the 1940s could have as many as 7,000 interdependent parts packed into a space the size of a hatbox. One change could affect thousands of manufacturing steps.

Consider a firm building antennas. How well its antenna works depends on transmitter power and receiver sensitivity. Other firms are developing those components, and neither is ready. The weakest component governs the performance of the entire machine, which makes it hard for any one company to justify investing on its own. This is what the authors call extreme interdependence. Many complementary parts have to advance together, and different organizations control them.

The authors describe three established ways of dealing with the problem. A vertically integrated firm, such as AT&T with Bell Labs, can coordinate internally. Much of the knowledge stays inside the firm. An R&D consortium such as the later SEMATECH shares costs, but members' incentives are hard to align and they retain most of the benefits. Decentralized markets circulate products freely and have more difficulty coordinating tightly integrated systems.

The Rad Lab centralized research in one building and organized it around radar's architecture. Separate groups worked on transmitting components, receiving components, and beacons, while others worked on airborne systems, ground and ship systems, navigation, and fire control. Manufacturing was distributed across many companies. A dedicated transfer office linked the researchers, military, and manufacturers. Contemporary records describe how the process worked. The systems division outlined a design, the component groups joined immediately, and company engineers worked alongside the lab's researchers. Work on development and preparation for volume production could overlap.

Standards give a concrete example. In the first two years of the war, component models proliferated, often with a different model for each project. Inventories became hard to manage, and prototypes required major redesigns for production. Midway through the war the Rad Lab set up an internal standards committee, brought manufacturers in, and later handed the work to the military. The authors identify four benefits. Components could come from different suppliers, multiple sources made supply more resilient, interchangeability made it easier to scale production, and repairs became easier. A coordinator made it possible to agree on standards across decentralized firms.

The authors define a coordinated R&D program by four features. One organization directs research. Production is spread among multiple firms. The coordinator actively manages relationships and handoffs among researchers, engineers, component suppliers, systems firms, and users. It also deliberately disseminates what the program produces beyond its own boundaries. This last feature is central to the paper.

## The lab made an unusual decision as the war drew to a close

By the end of the war, radar knowledge was highly classified and concentrated in the Rad Lab and its closest partners. Lab leaders realized that, left alone, the knowledge would effectively belong to a handful of large firms after the war. Associate director I. I. Rabi put it bluntly. Unless they wrote it down in books, Bell Labs would be the only place that understood the technology after the war.

Dissemination moved up the lab's list of priorities. The result was the 27-volume *MIT Radiation Laboratory Series*, which became a standard reference in the field. Together, the Rad Lab and its counterpart at Harvard, the Radio Research Laboratory (RRL), produced more than 2,500 patentable inventions, around 750 applications, and around 600 patents. Under the contracts, the U.S. government owned those patents. As far as the authors know, the government never asserted those rights against users.

This mattered in the scramble over patent rights. *Fortune* estimated that radar manufacturing drew on 2,000 to 3,000 basic patents, about 1,500 of which came out of the war. The government owned about one-third of the wartime patents, and companies held most of the rest. The Navy proposed a patent pool among the major companies. It would have favored incumbents and constrained entry, and it never got off the ground. The available evidence leaves the extent of the postwar patent thicket uncertain. Government ownership of many key patents may have made it less severe.

People were another outcome. The authors match the Rad Lab and RRL staff lists to patent inventors. They compare how those inventors and other inventors changed between the prewar period (1933–1940) and the postwar period (1949–1956). Two key results from Table 3 (p. 34) are below. “Radar patents” are patents in the six classes in which lab patents were most concentrated.

| Outcome, prewar to postwar | Coefficient (standard error) | Sample mean | Observations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of patents in radar classes | 0.091 (0.035) | 0.02 | 58,582 |
| Any patents in radar classes | 0.310 (0.062) | 0.04 | 58,582 |

Each coefficient compares the prewar-to-postwar change for former Rad Lab and RRL inventors with the change for other inventors over the same period. A coefficient of 0.310 means the probability of holding any radar-class patent rose about 31 percentage points more for lab alumni. The standard error in parentheses shows the uncertainty of the estimate. A coefficient generally needs to be about twice its standard error to be distinguishable from zero. Both estimates are above that threshold. The sample covers inventors who had patents in the prewar, wartime (1941–1948), and postwar periods, so it shows how people who kept inventing changed what they worked on. The comparison accounts for each inventor's fixed characteristics. The labs still selected their personnel, who may already have been more likely to move into new areas. Rad Lab alumni stayed in radar to a much greater extent after the war, by several times the sample mean. The appendix also shows that their postwar patents attracted more citations, a gap that had been absent before the war. By 1960, they were about 9.3 percentage points more likely to hold a management role than people in the same field with similar education and experience, against a mean of 26%.

## How three wartime outputs reached the postwar market

```text
Wartime Rad Lab output          Who used it after the war

Written knowledge            →  Wartime firms and new entrants.
(27 volumes, public patents)    No detectable difference by proximity to Cambridge.

Knowledge carried by people  →  Concentrated in wartime firms and near Cambridge.
(trained engineers)             Far fewer new entrants hired alumni.

Industry structure           →  Component suppliers led the postwar directories.
(suppliers and integrators)     The division of labor resembled the wartime one.
```

This diagram summarizes Section 5. Each arrow points to what the authors observed after the war. Consider a hypothetical electrical equipment firm that sat out the wartime radar project and wants to enter afterward. It can buy the books to learn microwave engineering and cite the Rad Lab's designs in its patents. It can use government-held patents without worrying about fees, while patents held by companies still pose a risk. It can start with a single component, such as a waveguide (a metal tube that carries microwaves), because the emerging market has a division of labor and interfaces to work with. Hiring a Rad Lab engineer is harder. Only about one in five postwar entrants hired someone with that background.

First consider the scale of the industry. The authors count firms under “Radar” in the *Thomas' Register of American Manufacturers*. They find about 50 in 1947 and around 175 in 1956. That is fewer than the “nearly 200” in the introduction, whose source is left unspecified. The authors suggest the directory may undercount the industry. In Figure 2, the number of wartime participants stays in the teens. Most growth comes from firms moving in from adjacent industries and from newly founded firms. The patents follow a similar pattern somewhat later. Wartime participants dominated the first few postwar years, and by 1960 companies from other industries were producing most radar patents (Figure 3).

The next question is how much these companies used the wartime program's outputs. The shares below come from Figure 4 (p. 32), measured in two-year groups, using the numbers given in the paper's discussion on p. 20.

| Among firms patenting in radar, at the peak | Wartime participants | Postwar entrants |
|---|---|---|
| At least one radar patent cites a Rad Lab patent or the book series | 60–80% | About 40% |
| Employed at least one former Rad Lab or RRL researcher | 50–60% | About 20% |

Wartime participants made more use of those outputs, as one would expect. Yet around 40% of firms that had never participated in the project cited them too. By the 1960s, technology had moved on and only 5% of radar patents cited a Rad Lab patent directly. Around half could still be traced back to one through at most three links in the citation chain.

The next test asks whether proximity to Cambridge mattered. Earlier research finds that knowledge tends to be used near where it was created, and engineers tend to move locally as well. The authors use having filed a radar patent in the prior year in Middlesex County, where MIT is located, as a proxy for local R&D. They compare it with three ways of using wartime outputs. The results below come from the three columns of Table 5 (p. 35) that include firm and year fixed effects. The comparison tracks the same firm in periods with and without local R&D and accounts for trends shared across years.

| Outcome | Coefficient on local R&D (standard error) | Sample mean |
|---|---|---|
| Cites a Rad Lab or RRL patent | 0.044 (0.076) | 0.32 |
| Cites the Rad Lab book series | 0.034 (0.061) | 0.21 |
| Employs a lab alumnus | 0.117 (0.048) | 0.26 |

The coefficients show differences in the probability of each outcome when the firm has local R&D. A coefficient of 0.117 is about 12 percentage points. Only the hiring result is distinguishable from zero, at roughly 2.4 times its standard error. Both citation coefficients are smaller than their standard errors, leaving room for sizable effects in either case. The authors take this pattern as evidence that the lab's deliberate effort to distribute written knowledge worked, while human capital remained local. The data fit that reading, and the analysis has no set of comparable knowledge that went undistributed to test it against. The proxy is also coarse. The hiring coefficient falls from 0.446 to 0.117 with fixed effects, suggesting that much of the initial difference reflects which firms chose to locate there. The direction fits the interpretation. The size deserves caution.

Finally, the authors manually classify businesses by their descriptions in the directory. Figure 5 (p. 33) shows component suppliers accounting for a little over 60% from 1947 through 1956. Systems firms fall from a little over 20% to between 10% and 20%, and services gain slightly. The most common terms are “Component,” “Equipment,” “Radar,” “Part,” and “Microwave,” in that order. The authors read this as continuity with the wartime structure. The industry described in the directory took a shape consistent with the wartime program, though the directory alone cannot establish inheritance. Another possibility, which the paper does not test, is that radar is inherently modular and the industry would have organized itself along similar lines regardless of who ran the project.

## How much depended on the war?

The obvious objection is that war made all of this possible. Urgency made it easier to experiment at scale, the military was the sole buyer and guaranteed demand, and some cooperation across firms and agencies might have raised antitrust concerns in peacetime.

The authors agree that these conditions accelerated development and deployment. They regard them as neither necessary nor sufficient for the commercial consequences. War explains why a project of this kind came to exist. What it left behind depended on whether its outputs were standardized, documented, and embedded in people and production relationships in ways firms competing in a regular postwar market could use.

The authors draw on three more recent cases from other researchers' work. They did not reexamine them themselves.

| Case | What happened | Comparison with radar |
|---|---|---|
| CCD image sensors for the Hubble Space Telescope | Several companies experimented, and patents and design techniques spread to civilian imaging | Similar at a much smaller scale. The CCD was only one input to the project. |
| Space Shuttle | A major technical achievement, with knowledge optimized for NASA's specific mission and deeply embedded in its organization and contractor network | A successful project whose knowledge did not spread directly. |
| ARPANET | Military-funded, with publicly documented protocols and a modular architecture that allowed outsiders to participate | Similar in that commercial impact depended on how open the program was. |

Taken together, these cases point to a way of judging the lasting value of a large project. What can people outside the project take away and use? The Space Shuttle offers the clearest comparison. It, too, was publicly led and technically successful.

## How far the evidence goes

The authors describe their approach as abductive. With one industry, one historical episode, and no counterfactual, they weigh the evidence as a whole instead of applying a standard causal design. They expressly caution against treating their findings as proof that the radar program created the postwar industry. Other paths might have produced similar results, and firms can organize ecosystems too. The closest thing to a counterfactual comes from an archival exchange. Vannevar Bush asked why manufacturers had not become independent of the lab sooner. The answer was that most lacked basic research capacity, enough engineers, close ties with the military, and test facilities.

There are further limits to keep in mind.

The directory's inclusion criteria are unclear, and the boundaries of the radar industry are fuzzy. The authors think the directory shows the right direction but may undercount firms. Patent data cover only firms that filed. Citations are a common but rough proxy for knowledge use. Engineer matching is also incomplete. Of roughly 2,400 employees, the authors could identify only about 800 in the engineering society directory.

The paper leaves open whether coordinated R&D outperforms the three alternatives. The authors use the word “conjecture,” and the four features are a definition drawn from one case. The appendix shows that lab patents were more novel and received more citations than comparable patents. In our view, that evidence cannot separate the effect of the way the program was organized from the effect of bringing top talent and enormous funding together.

The authors limit the framework to technical problems with highly interdependent parts. Where complementary components can develop independently, central coordination has less value. A question they raise for future work goes straight to a weak point of the framework. Under what conditions would a program become a barrier to entry and give its participants a permanent lead? Wartime firms did lead initially, and later entrants caught up. How much of that catch-up was due to government-held patents and the wide circulation of knowledge remains unresolved.

Return to the question of when governments can help create an industry. This case gives us a more precise question to ask when the project ends. What has been written down, learned by people who can take it elsewhere, or turned into interfaces others can use? The radar program had all three. Published knowledge spread widely, people stayed closer to the lab, and the industry that emerged followed a familiar wartime division of labor, though direct inheritance remains unproven. Could the same arrangement work today, with no war and no guaranteed demand? The authors say that takes more research.
